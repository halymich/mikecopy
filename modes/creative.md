# creative

Fiction, scripts, poetry, speeches, toasts, invitations, wedding and event wording. Same rules as
every other mode, em dashes included. That is the owner's decision: creative work does not get a
pass on the tells, and working without the em dash is a constraint that tends to improve rhythm.

## 1. Before a word

- **The occasion or the premise**, in one sentence.
- **The audience and the setting.** A toast is heard once, standing, after wine. A story is read
  alone. Write for the ear or the eye accordingly.
- **The form and its length.** Minutes for anything spoken (about 130 words a minute), words or
  lines for anything read. A sonnet, a limerick and free verse are different jobs.
- **The raw material.** For anything about real people (toasts, eulogies, wedding wording): names,
  how they met, the specific stories, the inside jokes that are safe to tell in public, and anything
  off limits. Ask for it. A toast built from generic warmth is the most obviously generated thing a
  person can stand up and read.
- **The tone.** From the voice record if there is one, otherwise [../core/tone.md](../core/tone.md).

## 2. Craft

- **Concrete over abstract.** The chipped blue mug, not "cherished memories".
- **Show the feeling through what people do and say.** Name it only when naming it is the surprise.
- **Earn the ending.** Leave one thing open. No moral stated out loud, no callback for its own sake.
- **Dialogue** dodges, interrupts and leaves things out. Nobody explains what both speakers know.
- **Poetry**: decide the form, then keep it. If it rhymes, the rhyme should land on a word that
  surprises. Metre is read aloud to check, not counted.
- **Spoken pieces**: short sentences, a line that can be delivered while looking up, and one laugh
  in the first minute if the occasion allows. Mark pauses for the speaker.
- **Invitations and event wording**: the facts (who, what, when, where, how to reply) are the
  piece. Formal wording follows the convention for the event's tradition; ask which one rather
  than guessing.

[../core/patterns.md](../core/patterns.md) lists the stock images and structures of generated
fiction. None of them belongs in a finished piece.

## 3. Offer versions, not one guess

For short pieces (a toast opening, a poem, invitation wording), give two or three versions that
differ in a real way (tone, form, angle) and say what each is doing. For long pieces, gate on a
one-paragraph premise or outline first.

## 4. Verify

Lint with `--stdin`, fix every hard finding, then read it aloud in your head once for rhythm. For
spoken pieces, report the estimated delivery time.
